Inner page design brief

The architecture in V1 is sound and most of it survives intact. This adds the live design system it was missing, assigns ground and colour per page, places the visual slots, and adds the two layers it has nothing on: mobile and conversion.

Verdict

Use it. The organising principle in §30 — don't design twelve service pages, design twelve business problems — is the right call and it is better articulated than anything I had written. Three other judgements in it are correct and worth protecting:

  • §2, tabs are not the page architecture. Right, and for the right reason: tabs hide content from the initial structure, which costs both comprehension and crawlability. The qualification in §32 — tabs only where the user is choosing between genuinely different states, like New Build vs Takeover — is the correct exception.
  • §26, don't chase fake Google features. Rare and correct.
  • §22, the service relationship map. Two named siblings and an insight per page is the anti-orphan problem actually solved rather than described.

One section has to be replaced wholesale before this goes to a designer, and two layers are missing. Everything below is that work.

How to read the two documents

Design gets both files. The architecture PDF carries the diagrams — the node sequences, the shell tree, the hub structures, the linking map, the URL tree — and this brief does not reproduce them. Read this brief first, then work from the PDF with the corrections below applied.

Two decisions in that sequence are doing real work:

  • “What you get” carries the page's own engine tint. Build pages get #FFE3A3, Grow pages #DDF2AC, Operate pages #B6E7DC. This is where the colour code earns its keep — a visitor who has seen the homepage already knows what the tint means, and the deliverables block is the section they came for.
  • “What stays yours” is the Petrol field. Ownership is the differentiator and the whole positioning. It gets the same treatment on every service page that it gets on the homepage, so the argument is recognisably the same one.
  • The “who it isn't for” half sits in an Ink panel inside a white section. The refusal is the most persuasive block on the page; a dark panel gives it weight without spending a section ground on it.

Hub pages

Each hub takes its own engine band as the hero ground — #FFF3D6 for /build, #EEF9D9 for /grow, #E0F4F0 for /operate — then White for the four service cards and Petrol for the CTA. Under 400 words, so one band is the whole colour budget. A hub that competes with its own service pages dilutes them.

Missing layer 1 — mobile

V1 has essentially nothing on mobile, and these pages will take the majority of their traffic from search on phones. The homepage was designed desktop-first and cost four rounds of rework; the service pages should not repeat it.

Every horizontal visual needs a named mobile form

The catalogue table, the customer journey, the Amazon/Walmart split and the control panel are all horizontal compositions. Specify the mobile version explicitly rather than letting it fall out of the CSS:

  • Catalogue table → stacked cards, one row per card, label-value pairs
  • Horizontal journey → vertical with the connector rotated, never a scroll-snap carousel
  • Split-screen comparison → stacked with a two-state toggle
  • Control panel → single column, one metric per row

The sticky section nav from §2 must be gated

A sticky element taller than innerHeight − offset hides its own bottom permanently and cannot be scrolled to. On this project that exact bug shipped once already. The section nav is sticky only above 900px and only when the viewport is tall enough; below that it is a collapsed “jump to” control or it is absent.

Above the fold on mobile

Breadcrumb, H1 naming the service, the first sentence of the sixty-word answer, one primary CTA. Nothing else. A 1,800–2,800 word page has one chance to say what it is.

Missing layer 2 — conversion

V1 covers SEO and GEO thoroughly and says almost nothing about what makes the page convert. Four additions, all structural rather than cosmetic.

Three CTA moments, not one

A closing CTA alone on a 2,000-word page wastes the two points where intent actually peaks. Place the same primary — Let's talk, identical label every time — after What you get (they now know the scope), after the first 90 days (they now trust the process), and at the close. Secondary See how we work stays a text-with-arrow, never a second button beside the primary.

Move the refusal higher, or mirror it

“Who this is for — and who it isn't” sits at position 3 of 8, roughly halfway down. On a trust-led page the refusal is the highest-converting block there is — it is the one thing a competitor cannot copy without meaning it. Keep the H2 where the spine puts it, but mirror one line of the disqualification in the hero, directly under the sixty-word answer. A visitor who self-selects out in the first screen was never going to convert, and one who reads it and stays has already qualified themselves.

Mobile sticky CTA bar

Appears after 50% scroll depth, one button, dismissible, never covering the FAQ accordions. This is the single highest-yield CRO element available on a long mobile page and it costs one component.

Keep /contact exactly as §18 specifies

Qualification fields, the “what are you looking to build” radio set, “what have you already tried”, then the three-step what happens next. That is a better contact page than most agencies ship and it should not be simplified into a name-and-email form.

Three conflicts to resolve before this goes out

1 · The §31 URL tree will be read as nesting

It draws /wholesale-ecommerce indented under /build, and /privacy under a /legal parent. Sections 5–10 give flat URLs (URL: /wholesale-ecommerce) and build-spec §J confirms flat, so the tree is a hierarchy diagram, not a URL structure — but a developer will implement it literally. Label it as hierarchy, and keep legal pages flat: /privacy /terms /accessibility /earnings-claims, not /legal/….

2 · /faq is a new page and a duplication risk

§19 adds it; build-spec §J's twenty-page sitemap does not contain it. Build-spec §O is explicit that FAQ blocks must never repeat across pages, because duplicate FAQ content is a self-inflicted problem. If /faq ships, the rule is: no question on /faq may appear on any service page or the homepage. It aggregates the questions that belong to no single service. If that leaves it thin, it should not exist.

3 · Word count versus visual density

§4 targets 1,800–2,800 words per service page. §32 asks for “maximum useful information with minimum visual density, not maximum words.” Those pull against each other, and a designer will feel it on the first layout. The resolution: the word target is a ceiling the FAQ absorbs, not a floor the body must reach. §4 already allocates 400–600 words to the FAQ — roughly a quarter — and FAQ is the cheapest place to be thorough without making the page feel long. Write the body to the shortest length that covers §O's requirements and let the FAQ carry the depth.

Update — the keyword map changed the page count

Added 1 Sep, after Keyword Map v2

Twenty-one live SERPs were read directly. Three things in that map change what design builds, and they are settled decisions, not open questions.

Ten service pages, not eleven — /dtc-growth is not built

Measured: it duplicates /shopify-dtc (tribe.studio, darkroomagency and forgedigitalmarketing rank on both terms) and its SERP rewards “best X agencies” roundups rather than service pages. The chip and footer entry stay and point at /shopify-dtc#growth, so the twelve-service taxonomy is untouched. Design consequence: the Shopify / DTC storefront-craft variant now covers /shopify-dtc and /ecommerce-website-development only, and /shopify-dtc gains a growth section with its own anchor.

/ecommerce-operations is a conversion page, not an acquisition page

Its natural primary term belongs to the homepage, and the obvious alternative resolves to offshore back-office outsourcing at $7–$42/hour. It stays a full service page on the standard template — it simply will not arrive from search. Design consequence: none to the template; it means this page's job is to convert a visitor already on the site, so the CTA moments matter more here than the hero does.

Two pages carry a measured objection that the design has to hold

On amazon seller account management, Google's own AI Overview cites Reddit advising readers not to hand an account to third-party management. On who owns my amazon seller account agency, the top Reddit answer is “don't trust any agencies”. That objection is now above every competitor in Google's voice. Design consequence: on /marketplace-management and /wholesale-ecommerce, “What stays yours” — the Petrol section — is not a reassurance block, it is the rebuttal to a stated objection. It should be the most designed section on those two pages, and it should be reachable from the hero.

Nothing else in this brief changes. The templates, grounds, tints, slot placements, mobile rules and conversion layer all stand.
